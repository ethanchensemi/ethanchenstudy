CPU / GPU / ASIC Wafer Cross-Section

Before packaging, look at the wafer: the full cross-section from solder bumps down to bulk silicon. All three chips share the same stack; the only difference is what circuit the transistors are wired into.
Left: the four zones; right: each layer by name. Bottom to top is the fab's build order: transistors first (FEOL), then copper wiring above (BEOL), then pads and solder bumps to hand off to packaging.
Die floorplans from above. All three have identical cross-sections — the difference is how die area is split between compute, on-die memory and I/O. In an AI ASIC the compute happens in the custom logic (MAC array); the SRAM is a scratchpad that feeds the array and does no math itself.

Solder bumps

the hand-off to packaging

Bumps are the last thing the fab adds and the first thing the OSAT or TSMC packaging line touches. They are not part of the wafer's own circuit — they are the interface to the substrate below.

Copper wiring (BEOL)

10 to 17 metal levels

Copper lines in low-k dielectric with tantalum barrier liners. Fine M1–M4 connect neighbouring transistors; thick top metal carries power and clock across the whole die. Contacts (tungsten/cobalt) at the bottom of this zone reach down to each transistor.

Transistors (FEOL)

fins, gates, source/drain

Silicon fins etched from the wafer, a hafnium-oxide gate dielectric a few atoms thick, a TiN/W metal gate, SiGe or SiP source/drain grown by epi. This is the layer that defines the node name (N3, N2, 18A).

Substrate (bulk Si)

Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers

The raw 300mm wafer. Only the top sliver ever becomes circuit; the rest is a mechanical carrier that gets thinned before packaging.

Which layer differs?

only what the transistors are wired to do

Cut a CPU, a GPU and an AI ASIC open and the cross-section is identical — same fins, same gate materials, same copper levels. What differs is the mask: which transistors connect to which. A CPU wires a few hundred transistors into a branch predictor; a GPU wires the same transistors into a multiply-add unit and repeats it thousands of times.

CPU

few complex cores, big cache, DDR off board

A handful of large cores built to run one thread fast, plus a large shared SRAM cache that hides the latency of DDR memory sitting far away on the motherboard. Package: flip-chip on ABF substrate, no interposer, no HBM.

GPU

thousands of simple cores, HBM on package

Thousands of small identical compute units. They generate so many simultaneous memory requests that DDR on the board cannot feed them, so HBM moves onto the package — which is the entire reason CoWoS exists.

AI ASIC

one job, custom blocks, HBM on package

A few purpose-built logic blocks doing one kind of math, on-die SRAM buffers, and much of the perimeter given to SerDes and HBM I/O. Designed by Broadcom, Marvell, Alchip or GUC for the hyperscalers; same wafer stack, same CoWoS package as a GPU.

Two bonding routes inside SoIC. The difference is when stacking happens: WoW aligns and bonds whole wafers first and then dices; CoW dices first and then places dies one by one onto the target wafer.

03A　Wafer-on-Wafer (WoW)

Wafer to wafer

Two complete wafers, each already holding many chip circuits, are aligned and bonded whole. Only after bonding is the stack diced into individual chips.
Both wafers must match exactly in size and layout, because every site is aligned and bonded at once.
Two complete wafers are bonded face to face (Cu–Cu hybrid bonding), and only then cut along the dicing lanes into individual stacks.

Best fit

This works best when both wafers have the same chip size and layout, since everything on the wafer lines up at once.

03B　Chip-on-Wafer (CoW)

Chip to wafer

Chips are diced from the wafer first, then placed and bonded one by one onto the target wafer — more flexible when mixing die sizes or screening for good dies.
Different die sizes can be mixed, and dies that failed test can be skipped — only known good dies are used.
The source wafer is diced and tested first; only known good dies (KGD) are placed and bonded one by one onto the target wafer, which is then diced.

Advantage

This is more flexible, since you can mix chips of different sizes, or skip over any that failed testing, only using known good dies.

04

Fan-out: Wafer Level vs. Panel Level

FOWLP vs. FOPLP

The same fan-out packaging concept; the only difference is whether it is built on a round wafer or a square panel.
Built on a round 300mm wafer; square package sites around the rim fall partly off the curved edge and are scrapped.
End uses: smartphone SoCs (Apple A-series via InFO), application processors, RF transceivers, PMICs, connectivity chips, automotive sensors
The same fan-out package built on a large square panel: no curved-edge waste and far more units per run.
End uses: PMICs, display driver ICs, RF and connectivity chips, some automotive and industrial parts

FOWLP

Fan-out packaging built on a round 300mm wafer, where square package sites around the perimeter fall partly off the curved edge and are scrapped.

FOPLP

The same fan-out packaging built on a large rectangular panel, which has no curved edge to waste and holds far more units per run — provided yield and warpage across the larger format can be controlled.

InFO-PoP vs. FOWLP / FOPLP: structure and carrier are two independent choices

InFO-PoP describes what is inside the package: the SoC, the through-mould vias, the fan-out RDL and the DRAM stacked on top. FOWLP and FOPLP describe what it is built on: a round wafer or a square panel. The two choices are independent, so the same InFO-PoP design can in principle be produced on either carrier. Volume production today is wafer level.

  • Structure (what it is): InFO-PoP, InFO-oS, InFO-LSI, CoWoS…
  • Carrier format (what it is built on): FOWLP (wafer) or FOPLP (panel)
  • The two are orthogonal: "a wafer-level package built on a square panel" is a contradiction, because wafer-level is itself the name of the carrier format
05

QFN and RF Chips: From Wafer to QFN Package

QFN packaging — how an RF chip is built, wafer to package, side view

The other end of the spectrum from advanced packaging: the cheapest, most traditional leadframe package. Nearly all RF, analog and power chips use it. Left half is wafer level, right half is package level.
Left: RF wafer level (InP or GaAs substrate → epi → transistors → dice). Right: QFN cross-section (die on the leadframe pad, wire-bonded to the leads, moulded, flat on the PCB). Numbers match the eight steps below.

① Substrate

InP or GaAs wafer

A compound-semiconductor wafer — GaAs for phones and WiFi at low voltage, GaN-on-Si for base stations and radar at high voltage, InP for lasers. Suppliers: AXT, Xiamen Powerway, SCIOCS.

② Epi layer

MOCVD

Grown by MOCVD on the substrate. For GaN the epi is usually GaN on a cheap silicon substrate — the GaN layer does the electrical work, the silicon is only a carrier. Suppliers: IQE, VPEC, IntelliEPI.

③ Devices

RF transistors patterned into the epi

RF transistors are patterned into the epi layer. Foundries: WIN Semiconductors, AWSC (GaAs), PSMC (GaN-on-Si), Sanan IC; IDMs like Qorvo, Skyworks and Infineon fabricate in-house.

④ Dice

into RF dies

The wafer is cut into individual RF dies — one die per package, unlike the multi-die CoWoS stacks.

⑤ Wire bond

die to leadframe

The die is glued onto the leadframe's central pad and connected to the leads with thin gold or copper wires arcing from the top of the die. No flip-chip bumps, no substrate.

⑥ Mould

compound over die and wires

Mould compound is poured over the die and wires and cured, forming the black plastic body. Tools: TOWA.

⑦ Leads and pad

exposed on the underside

The leadframe leads and the exposed thermal pad are the only metal on the bottom — the pad sits directly under the die.

⑧ Solder

flat to the PCB

Leads and pad are soldered flat to the PCB: no balls, no substrate, no socket. OSATs: ASE, Amkor, JCET. Heat exits downward through the pad into the PCB.

Advanced Packaging Family: Full Cross-Sections

Advanced Packaging Family — Apple SiP and InFO-PoP, Intel and Samsung, TSMC variants

The interposer is the key difference across this family. First the mobile structures that need no interposer, SiP and InFO-PoP; then Intel's and Samsung's counterparts, EMIB and I-Cube; finally TSMC's own two variants, CoWoP and CoPoS. Under each cross-section is the carrier it is built on: round wafer, square panel, or substrate strip.
The interposer　A thin layer, usually silicon, sandwiched between the chips above (a GPU or CPU alongside HBM memory) and the layers below. Its job is to route extremely fine wiring between chips sitting side by side, because those chips need to communicate at very high speed and density — something the layer beneath cannot do on its own.
A　Mobile: Apple SiP and InFO-PoPTwo structures with no interposer, often used together
Substrate strip　assembled on an organic substrate strip (substrate level, not wafer level)
Logic die, PMIC and DRAM sit side by side on one shared substrate — the simplest of the structures.
Round wafer　built on a 300mm silicon wafer (wafer level)
DRAM is stacked on top through PoP solder balls; the SoC (CPU, GPU and ASIC blocks) sits in fan-out mould compound and connects straight to the fan-out RDL — no interposer or bridge anywhere.
B　Intel and Samsung: EMIB / I-CubeThe other two players' equivalents: a bridge embedded in the substrate, or a full silicon interposer
Substrate strip　assembled on an organic substrate strip (substrate level, not wafer level)
No full interposer: a small silicon bridge is embedded in the substrate, only under the seam between dies.
Round wafer　built on a 300mm silicon wafer (wafer level)
Structurally identical to CoWoS — Samsung's own silicon-interposer packaging.
C　TSMC variants: CoWoP / CoPoSSame upper and lower layers as CoWoS: one drops the substrate, one swaps the interposer for a panel
Round wafer　built on a 300mm silicon wafer (wafer level)
The ABF substrate is omitted and the interposer mounts directly on the PCB, which usually has to be SLP grade to make up for the missing substrate.
Square panel　built on a glass or sapphire panel (panel level)
Same structure as CoWoS, but a panel-level RDL (glass or sapphire base) replaces the silicon interposer; the ABF substrate stays.
Materials: CoWoS-R vs. CoPoS

CoWoS-R's RDL is polymer dielectric and copper wiring on a round silicon wafer, while CoPoS's RDL is built on a glass or sapphire panel instead, a genuinely different base material, not just a different shape.

07

CoWoS Three Variants: S / L / R, Side and Top Views

CoWoS-S, CoWoS-L, CoWoS-R — side view and top view

The CoWoS in the previous section is the generic drawing; TSMC actually has three variants, differing in interposer material and how much of it is silicon. Top row side view, bottom row top view.

CoWoS-S

full silicon interposer, reticle-limited

One continuous silicon interposer with TSVs under all dies. Highest wiring density, but the interposer cannot exceed one reticle field (~830 mm²), which caps how many HBM stacks fit. H100/H200, MI250, earlier TPU.

CoWoS-L

RDL + local silicon bridges, the growth variant

An organic RDL interposer with small silicon bridges embedded only under the die-to-die and die-to-HBM seams. Silicon goes only where high-speed wiring is needed, so the package can exceed the reticle. Blackwell, Rubin, MI300–MI455X, TPU via Broadcom, Trainium3, Maia.

CoWoS-R

organic RDL only, cheapest, lowest density

No silicon at all — the interposer is pure organic RDL. Cheapest and not reticle-limited, but the wiring is coarser, so it suits lower-bandwidth HBM attach and some networking silicon rather than flagship GPUs.

How to tell them apart　Look at the interposer material: S is full silicon, L is RDL plus local silicon bridges, R is pure RDL with no silicon. The upper layers (dies + HBM) and lower layers (ABF substrate → PCB) are identical across all three; only the middle layer differs. In the top views, blue marks silicon coverage: all of it for S, only the seams for L, none for R.

EMIB 3.5D — Foveros + EMIB (Co-EMIB announced 2019, Ponte Vecchio 2023)

3.5D — Foveros stacks joined by EMIB bridges

Two or more Foveros stacks (top dies on base dies) sit side by side on the substrate, and EMIB bridges in the substrate link the base dies to each other: vertical density from Foveros, lateral reach from EMIB, beyond the reticle limit. Ponte Vecchio (Data Center GPU Max) is the reference example; the earlier name was Co-EMIB. Counterpart of TSMC 3DFabric (SoIC + CoWoS).

① Foveros Direct — hybrid bonding (announced 2021)

3D — copper-to-copper hybrid bonding

Same stacking as Foveros but the micro-bumps are removed: copper pads on the two dies bond directly, pitch under 10µm. This is the true equivalent of TSMC SoIC and of the HBM4 hybrid-bonding transition. Highest density, tightest tolerances.

② Foveros Omni — copper columns and overhang (2021)

3D — die overhang and copper columns (announced 2021)

Announced in 2021 as the next generation of Foveros: the top die can be larger than the base die and overhang it, with copper columns moulded in beside the base carrying power straight from the substrate to the top die, so dies from different nodes and sizes can be mixed freely; bump pitch 25µm. Intel Foundry's current catalogue lists Foveros as S/R/B 2.5D plus Direct 3D, so the Omni name no longer appears as a separate product — its copper-column power delivery carried into the later Foveros generations.

① Foveros-B — RDL with bridges (announced 2025, production 2026)

2.5D — RDL interposer with embedded silicon bridges

An RDL interposer with small silicon bridges embedded under the seams between dies, giving silicon-class density only where the high-speed links are. Production-ready 2026. Intel's answer to CoWoS-L — and structurally different from EMIB, whose bridge sits one layer lower, inside the substrate.

② Foveros-R — RDL interposer (announced 2025, production 2026)

2.5D — organic RDL interposer, no silicon

Same side-by-side placement, but the interposer is an organic redistribution layer instead of silicon: cheaper and not reticle-limited, at lower wiring density. Aimed at client and cost-sensitive parts. Production-ready 2026. Intel's answer to CoWoS-R.

③ Foveros-S — silicon interposer (in production 2019)

2.5D — dies side by side on a silicon base die with TSVs

Chiplets are bonded face to face onto a silicon base die that carries TSVs. Intel counts it as 2.5D because the top dies sit side by side on that interposer. Micro-bump pitch 36µm; the base die can be active (I/O, cache) rather than a passive interposer. In production since 2019 — Lakefield, then Meteor Lake and Arrow Lake. Interposer up to about 4× reticle.

① EMIB-T — TSVs in the bridge (announced 2024, production 2026)

EMIB with through-silicon vias in the bridge

The same embedded bridge, but the bridge die now carries TSVs, so power and ground can pass vertically through it instead of being routed around it. Aimed at HBM4-class bandwidth and packages with many bridges, where the plain EMIB power path becomes the limit.

② EMIB-M — MIM capacitors in the bridge (announced 2025)

EMIB with MIM capacitors in the bridge

The same embedded bridge, with metal-insulator-metal decoupling capacitors built into the bridge die. They sit right under the die-to-die link, so supply noise is filtered where it matters and the substrate's power network is relieved. One of the two current production variants, alongside EMIB-T.

③ EMIB — baseline (in production 2017) — Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge

2.5D — bridge in the substrate, no interposer

Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge. A small silicon bridge is buried inside the ABF substrate under each seam, so there is no interposer layer at all. Intel's answer to CoWoS-L, with the bridge one layer lower. Used in Intel data-center parts; Amazon is a reported packaging customer.

InFO Five Variants, Grouped by Substrate

TSMC InFO — five common variants, grouped by whether there is a substrate

What the InFO family shares: a fan-out RDL and no silicon interposer. Sort them first by whether there is a substrate: PoP, AiP and SoW have none, the RDL connects straight to the PCB; oS and LSI put the RDL on an ABF substrate. These are the five common variants.
A　No substrate: RDL straight to the PCBNewest first: SoW (2020), AiP (2018), PoP (2016)
B　On substrate: RDL mounted on an ABF substrateNewest first: LSI (2020), oS (2017)
What the InFO family shares　All five use a fan-out RDL and none has a silicon interposer. First ask whether there is a substrate: PoP, AiP and SoW have none, the RDL goes straight to the PCB (a system board for SoW); oS and LSI put the RDL on an ABF substrate. Then look at what each adds: PoP stacks memory on top, AiP puts the antenna on top, SoW never dices the wafer, LSI embeds a bridge in the RDL. All InFO is done in-house at TSMC, not outsourced to OSATs.

InFO-SoW (announced 2020)

system on wafer — the wafer is never diced

Dozens of dies are placed across a whole reconstituted wafer and joined by a wafer-wide fan-out RDL; the wafer is not cut into packages. Power and I/O connect straight to the wafer, which then mounts on a system board. Tesla's Dojo training tile (25 D1 dies) is this structure.

InFO-AiP (announced 2018)

antenna in package — mmWave RF

The antenna array is patterned into RDL on top of the moulded package, with the RF transceiver die inside and the fan-out RDL underneath. Antenna and radio become one part, which matters at mmWave where the antenna-to-chip distance has to be tiny. No substrate.

InFO-PoP (in production 2016)

fan-out package-on-package, no substrate

The SoC sits in mould compound with through-InFO vias (TIV) at the edges; fan-out RDL underneath replaces the substrate; a DRAM package stacks on top through the TIVs. DRAM goes on top (not beside) because near memory needs a wide, short path. iPhone A-series since A10; Apple Watch S-series, where the InFO-PoP is nested inside a larger SiP module.

InFO-LSI (announced 2020, qualified 2021)

local silicon interconnect inside the RDL

A small silicon bridge (LSI) is embedded inside the fan-out RDL under the seam between two large dies, joining them as one. Structurally the fan-out cousin of CoWoS-L bridges. Apple M1/M2 Ultra “UltraFusion” is this.

InFO-oS (in production 2017)

fan-out RDL on substrate

Fan-out RDL built first, then mounted on an ABF substrate — oS means “on Substrate”. Gives finer wiring than a substrate alone without a silicon interposer. Used for high-I/O networking and some HPC chips without HBM.

Apple SiP in Depth: iPhone Logic Board vs. Apple Watch SiP Module

Apple SiP in depth — where the InFO-PoP sits in an iPhone versus an Apple Watch

A SiP (System-in-Package) seals a whole small system into one module; the InFO-PoP is the processor package inside it. The iPhone solders the InFO-PoP straight onto the logic board; the Apple Watch nests it inside the S-series SiP module. Both products use the same InFO-PoP — the difference is whether another layer is wrapped around it.
iPhone: the logic board itself is the "system". The InFO-PoP (A-series SoC with DRAM stacked on top), PMIC, NAND, modem and RF module are each packaged separately and soldered side by side onto the PCB.
Apple Watch: the whole system is assembled on one module substrate and moulded into the S-series SiP; the InFO-PoP is just one component inside it. The module is then soldered to the watch board.

What a SiP is

System-in-Package — a module, not a die-level technology

A SiP puts the processor, memory, storage, wireless, power management and passives side by side on one module substrate, then moulds the whole thing into a single part. It is a system-level package built by an OSAT, not a TSMC front-end technology; TSMC's part ends at the InFO-PoP inside it.

What the InFO-PoP does inside

the processor package inside — TSMC-made, wafer level

The InFO-PoP is the SoC with its DRAM stacked on top through TIVs and a fan-out RDL underneath, built by TSMC on a 300mm reconstituted wafer. In an iPhone it is the biggest package on the board; in a Watch it is one component among several inside the SiP.

Why the Watch uses a SiP and the iPhone doesn't

space, sealing and testability vs. board room and supplier flexibility

A watch has almost no board area and must survive water and shock, so one moulded module that is tested as a unit and dropped onto the board wins. A phone has room on the logic board and Apple prefers to keep the modem and RF front end as separate packages it can source and swap independently.

What it means for the supply chain

who captures which layer

TSMC keeps the InFO-PoP in house (wafer level, not outsourced). The SiP module assembly for the Watch goes to OSATs (ASE group among others), on a laminate module substrate. For the iPhone there is no SiP layer at all: the packages go straight to the EMS line (Foxconn and others) for board-level SMT.

CPO: COUPE Optical Engine and Package Build

Co-Packaged Optics — COUPE's three stacked technologies, then the full build from InP wafer to CPO package

Top: SoIC is used not only to stack compute dies but also to bond the EIC (electronic IC) to the PIC (photonic IC) into an optical engine, which then shares a CoWoS interposer with the ASIC. Bottom: the full build sequence, laser wafer level on the left, package level on the right.
A　COUPE optical engineThree technologies stacked at once
The optical engine (EIC + PIC) is itself a SoIC-made package, treated as a chiplet and placed on the same CoWoS interposer as the customer ASIC.

1　SoIC

Hybrid-bond the 65nm electronic IC (EIC) to the photonic IC (PIC) to make the optical engine.

2　CoWoS

Place the optical engine and the customer ASIC on the same interposer.

3　CPO

Result: optics move inside the package, replacing the pluggable optical modules outside it.

B　CPO package build: InP wafer to CPO packageHow a CPO switch is built — laser wafer level on the left, package level on the right
Left: laser wafer level (InP substrate → epi → laser structure → dice). Right: CPO package level (optical engine → placement → fibre attach → board). Numbers match the eight steps below.

① Substrate

InP wafer

An indium phosphide wafer grown as the base material — a compound semiconductor, not silicon. Suppliers: SCIOCS (Sumitomo), AXT.

② Epi layer

MOCVD on InP

A thin crystalline layer grown by MOCVD on the InP substrate. This is the layer that will emit light. Suppliers: IQE, IntelliEPI, Landmark.

③ Laser device

patterned into the epi

The laser diode structure is patterned into the epi layer. Same sequence as an RF chip — different material, same process shape.

④ Dice

into laser dies

The wafer is cut into individual laser dies, ready to be combined with the photonic IC.

⑤ Optical engine

PIC + EIC, often via SoIC

The photonic IC (PIC — waveguides, modulators; designed by Broadcom, Marvell, Nvidia, Ayar Labs; fabbed at TSMC, GlobalFoundries, Tower) is stacked on the electronic IC (EIC — drivers, TIAs, DSP; Broadcom, Marvell, MACOM, Semtech), usually by SoIC hybrid bonding. The laser either sits with the PIC or stays outside as an external laser source (ELS).

⑥ Placement

beside the switch ASIC

The optical engines are placed beside the switch ASIC on one package substrate (or CoWoS interposer); a lid and coldplate go over the ASIC. Heat exits upward — the opposite of QFN.

⑦ Fiber attach

FAU → MPO

A fiber array unit (FAU — Orbray, ACON, Corning, Senko, Fujikura) aligns the fibers to the PIC; the fiber exits the package to an MPO (Multi-fiber Push-On) connector — the part a technician handles.

⑧ Finish

BGA to the switch board

The substrate is mounted to the switch board on BGA balls. Assembly: TSMC (COUPE), ASE, Amkor; substrate: Ibiden; test: ficonTEC, Chroma.

Why the laser often stays outside the package　Lasers are temperature-sensitive and the switch ASIC runs hot. Making the laser an external light source (ELS) and piping the light in by fibre to the PIC inside the package keeps it away from the ASIC's heat. This is also why the CPO supply chain spans both the RF side (InP epi, lasers) and the GPU side (substrate, CoWoS, lid).

NVL72 Compute Rack (18 trays)

NVL72 Compute Tray — 18 trays per rack

The rack holds 18 compute trays. Below: the structure of one tray and the related vocabulary.
2 Grace CPUs, 4 B200 GPUs, 4 network chips, 2 DPUs, SSDs and M.2, 1 BMC. Top of the diagram = rear of the rack (NVLink midplane); bottom = front panel (network and service ports).

Grace CPU

NVIDIA's Arm-based server CPU (72 Neoverse V2 cores) with LPDDR5X memory on its own module. Two per tray, one in each GB200 superchip; it feeds the GPUs and runs the host software rather than doing the AI math.

Blackwell GPU (B200)

The AI accelerator: two reticle-sized dies joined on a CoWoS-L interposer with eight HBM3E stacks beside them. Four per tray, each under its own cold plate — the single largest consumer of power and cooling on the board.

GB200 Superchip

One Grace CPU plus two Blackwell GPUs on a single board module, linked by NVLink-C2C so the CPU and GPUs share memory coherently. A tray holds two superchips, which is where the 2 CPU + 4 GPU count comes from.

NVLink connectors / midplane (rear)

Rear blind-mate connectors that carry NVLink over copper to the rack's NVLink switch trays. This is the scale-up fabric: it makes all 72 GPUs in the rack behave as one shared-memory domain. Copper, not optical, because the reach is only within the rack.

ConnectX network card

The network chip handling high-speed AI traffic between GPUs across trays.

BlueField DPU

A separate chip handling storage and management traffic to systems outside the rack.

OSFP transceiver

The physical connector module that plugs a network chip into fiber optic cable, carrying 8 channels for very high speed, 400G or 800G.

10GbE LAN port

A network connector running at 10 gigabits per second, likely used for management or storage traffic separate from the main AI data path.

Power connector (rear)

Rear blind-mate connector that draws DC from the rack busbar when the tray is pushed in; the rack's power shelves have already converted AC to DC upstream. No individual power supplies inside the tray.

Power Distribution Board

Takes the busbar DC and steps it down and distributes it to the voltages the CPUs, GPUs, NICs and drives need. It is the reason the tray needs no separate PSU.

Cold water inlet / hot water outlet (rear)

Quick-disconnect couplings for the liquid loop: coolant enters cold, passes through the cold plates on the CPUs and GPUs, and leaves warm to the rack manifold and on to the CDU. Also blind-mates on insertion.

Manifold

The piping connection point where the rack's liquid cooling loop plugs into the tray.

Fan modules

Even in a liquid-cooled tray, fans still move air across the parts that have no cold plate — NICs, DPUs, SSDs, voltage regulators. Far smaller and fewer than in an air-cooled server.

Battery

A coin cell that keeps the real-time clock and management settings alive when the tray is unpowered. Housekeeping only — it plays no part in powering the system.

BMC — Baseboard Management Controller (Aspeed)

The management chip that runs independently of the host: monitors temperatures, fans and voltages, powers the tray on and off remotely, and updates firmware. The Aspeed part is the same Taiwanese BMC silicon used across most servers.

BMC 1GbE port / USB / VGA (front)

The BMC's own network port for out-of-band management, plus USB and VGA for a technician to plug in a keyboard and screen locally. Separate from the AI data path and from the 10GbE port.

HMC — Host Management Controller

Works alongside the BMC to provide out-of-band hardware management for the tray.

TPM — Trusted Platform Module

A dedicated security chip that stores encryption keys and verifies the system hasn't been tampered with during boot.

M.2 NVMe

A small SSD used as the boot drive holding the operating system.

Cache storage (SSD ×4)

Front-loaded NVMe SSDs used as local scratch and cache for datasets and checkpoints, separate from the M.2 boot drive. Front-mounted so they can be swapped without pulling the tray.
